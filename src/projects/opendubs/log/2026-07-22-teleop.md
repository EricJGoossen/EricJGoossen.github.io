To give the arms demonstrations and a way to drive them in real time, I built teleoperation with a Meta Quest. Earlier pose sources needed an app installed on the headset, so I replaced them with a page served from the robot: the Quest's own browser opens it over HTTPS, enters AR, and streams both controllers' poses, grip and trigger over a websocket. The bridge has the same functions as the SDK it replaced, so switching sources was a one-line import change, and any other browser on the network that opens the page becomes a live diagnostics view of what the headset is sending.

Squeezing a controller's grip engages its arm, so the arm follows the hand only while it's held, and the trigger opens and closes the gripper, with a hysteresis band so one squeeze can't register twice. I built it against the arms in MuJoCo first, then added support for the real arms the next day. At first the loop couldn't keep up, and the bottleneck wasn't the control at all but publishing the simulated viewer over the network. Beyond that, speed is configurable: a cap on how far the gripper target can move per tick and on its Cartesian speed, the loop rate, a switch between running the loop synchronously and asynchronously, and gripper commands that no longer block the loop while the gripper moves. With the arms mounted on the finished base, teleop first ran on the robot on September 24, the morning of the Winbond demo.

Along the way, the analytical IK solver failed on the OpenArm's geometry. I wrote scripts to sweep and correlate the failures, traced them to the arm's zero-length links, which make the solver's equations degenerate, and patched the solver to nudge those lengths by a tiny fraction of the arm's scale. I opened the fix upstream; the maintainer fixed the same bug before it merged, and confirmed my patch would have worked.
